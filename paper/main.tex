\begin{abstract}
Most competition working notes describe a winning recipe. This note deliberately
does the opposite, and argues the inversion is useful: a systematic,
measurement-driven account of \emph{why a strong shared public baseline could not
be beaten with consumer-scale resources}, and what that reveals about the
structure of modern bioacoustic competitions. By mid-competition a single
architectural pattern --- a frozen Perch~v2 embedding model, a Light-ProtoSSM
sequence head, a distilled Sound-Event-Detection (SED) CNN, and a
taxonomy-smoothing post-processor --- had been openly shared and converged on a
public-leaderboard score of \textbf{0.950}, where (as of the public-LB snapshot
on 2~June~2026) roughly \textbf{890 of 4243 teams scored in the
$[0.950, 0.951)$ band}. We treat this plateau as an object of study rather than a
target. We used a disciplined \emph{local cross-validation gate} --- a
leak-controlled offline split scored before spending one of five daily submission
slots --- to test every lever available to a small team: own-trained SEDs,
foundation-model linear probes, rare-taxon specialists, post-hoc calibration
tricks, and architecturally-diverse model ensembles. Each was neutral or negative.
Two observations we found practically consequential in this setting: (1)~the public
components' apparent near-perfect held-out accuracy is \emph{optimistic by
construction} --- they are fit on the only in-domain labelled audio available for
validation --- which inverts the most natural offline comparison and makes held-out
macro-AUC an unreliable basis for ranking a challenger against the baseline; and
(2)~the binding constraint for a small team is not modeling skill but
\textbf{platform limits} --- a ${\sim}1$~MB notebook-size cap and a 90-minute CPU
runtime budget --- which physically forbid the one technique with genuine headroom
(ensembling multiple full foundation-model pipelines). On a single held-out split,
architecturally-diverse CNN ensembling improved macro-AUC by \textbf{+0.021} over
its best member (a leak-controlled, directional result, \S\ref{sec:ensemble}),
yet that ensemble could not be deployed within the runtime envelope. We close
with a hypothesis, offered explicitly as conjecture since the leaders' solutions
are private: in the foundation-model era a public pipeline assembled from large
pretrained components plus a community-discovered post-processing trick is a
remarkably hard floor, and we conjecture the residual gap to the leaders is paid
largely in up-front training compute rather than inference-time cleverness. All
code and the offline-gate harness are released openly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

BirdCLEF+ 2026, the bird-focused task of the LifeCLEF 2026 evaluation
campaign~\cite{lifeclef2026,birdclef2026overview}, asked participants to identify
which of 234 species --- birds, amphibians, mammals, reptiles, and insects ---
vocalize in 1-minute soundscapes from the Brazilian Pantanal, an important
conservation-monitoring task. Submissions are CPU-only Kaggle notebooks evaluated
on a hidden set of ${\sim}600$ recordings, scored by macro-averaged ROC-AUC that
skips classes with no true positives in the test set; the runtime budget is
\textbf{90 minutes}, internet is disabled, and each team may submit five times per
day.

The competition exhibited an unusually sharp \emph{public-kernel monoculture}. A
lineage of openly-shared notebooks (``Ensemble-of-Solutions'', EoS.x, and their
forks) raised the public ceiling in discrete, community-wide jumps ---
$0.928 \to 0.948 \to 0.949 \to \textbf{0.950}$ --- each jump driven by one new
mechanism that the field adopted within days. The cumulative public-LB
distribution (snapshot 2~June~2026, 4243 teams) was top-heavy and then sharply
flat: \textbf{11 teams $\geq 0.96$}, \textbf{42 $\geq 0.955$},
\textbf{252 $\geq 0.951$}, and \textbf{1140 $\geq 0.950$} --- i.e.\ ${\sim}890$
teams whose best public score fell in the narrow $[0.950, 0.951)$ band, the large
population running the shared pipeline essentially unchanged. (Public-LB counts
drift as teams resubmit; we report one dated snapshot.)

We entered this monoculture late and spent the competition on one question:
\emph{can a small team, on a single consumer GPU plus a small cloud budget, add
anything to the public 0.950 pipeline that survives contact with the hidden test?}
Our answer is negative. Each hypothesis was reduced to a measured quantity and
gated before a submission was spent. The contribution of this note is therefore
not a model but a \textbf{map}: what we tried, the numbers, the structural reason
each path failed, and a small reusable methodology for resource-constrained
competitors. Negative results reported with measurements are comparatively rare
in this venue, since most write-ups come from teams that succeeded; we hope the
surrounding terrain is useful.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

BirdCLEF has run annually since 2014; recent editions converged on
transfer-learning from large audio/bird foundation models followed by sequence
modeling and heavy ensembling of diverse CNNs (the consistent ingredient in
winning solutions across 2023--2025)~\cite{lifeclef-overview}. Our public
baseline reflects this: Perch~v2~\cite{perch} as the embedding backbone, a
ProtoSSM-style selective-state-space~\cite{mamba} head over its embeddings, and a
distilled SED CNN. Our own-model attempts draw on noisy-student
self-training~\cite{noisystudent} (pseudo-labeling unlabeled soundscapes with a
teacher, then training a student) and on foundation-model linear probing
(Bird-MAE~\cite{birdmae}, AudioMAE~\cite{audiomae}). Two themes we engage
critically are (i)~knowledge distillation's ceiling~\cite{distillation} --- a
student trained on a teacher's outputs is bounded by that teacher --- and
(ii)~ensemble diversity --- that decorrelated architectures, averaged, beat any
single member. Our contribution relative to this literature is empirical and
negative: we quantify \emph{where each of these reliably-useful techniques stops
working} under this competition's specific data-leakage and platform constraints.

\section{Anatomy of the public 0.950 pipeline}
\label{sec:anatomy}

The converged public solution is a four-stage rank ensemble:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Perch~v2}~\cite{perch} --- Google's bird-vocalization foundation
        model --- produces a 1536-d embedding and 234-logit score per 5-second
        window. It is the single most expensive inference stage and the dominant
        signal.
  \item \textbf{Light-ProtoSSM} --- a small (${\approx}0.75$--$5.8$~M-parameter)
        bidirectional selective-state-space~\cite{mamba} head with prototype
        classification and cross-window attention --- is fit (or loaded
        pre-trained) on Perch embeddings of the labelled soundscapes, contributing
        ${\sim}60\%$ of the rank blend.
  \item \textbf{Distilled SED} --- a 5-fold EfficientNet-B0 sound-event detector on
        $256 \times 313$ log-mel input, distilled~\cite{distillation} from a
        stronger teacher ensemble, contributing ${\sim}40\%$.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{TAX\_SMOOTHING}} --- the final-jump mechanism
        ($0.949 \to 0.950$): each species' score is pulled slightly toward the mean
        of its genus- and class-siblings ($\alpha_{\text{genus}} \approx 0.15$,
        $\alpha_{\text{class}} \approx 0.05$), exploiting taxonomic correlation
        among co-occurring Pantanal taxa.
\end{enumerate}

We verified directly that every public 0.950 notebook is a scalar-tuned variant of
this stack: forks differing in post-processing (BirdNET sidecars, PCEN,
out-of-fold gating, iterative smoothing) and even one with a different ProtoSSM
implementation all returned exactly 0.950 or below.

\section{Method: local cross-validation as a pre-submission gate}
\label{sec:method}

The scarce resources here are not FLOPs but \textbf{submission slots} (five per
day, each scored only after a multi-hour hidden-test rerun) and \textbf{trustworthy
validation signal} (the labelled audio is tiny and, as \S\ref{sec:trap} shows,
optimistically biased toward the public models). Our central methodological move
was to never spend a slot on an unmeasured hypothesis: for each idea we built a
cheap offline test on cloud GPU (Modal) or the local GPU and used it as a
go/no-go \emph{gate} on the submission budget.

\subsection{The offline split, and how it is kept leak-controlled}
\label{sec:split}

At bottom this is a \emph{local cross-validation split} of the kind familiar to
Kaggle competitors --- hold out labelled data, score a candidate on it offline,
and promote it to a real submission only if it clears a bar. What we add is
(i)~treating that split as a \emph{hard gate} on a five-per-day budget rather than
a diagnostic weighed against intuition, and (ii)~making it \emph{leak-aware},
because in this competition the most natural local split silently favours the
public baseline (\S\ref{sec:trap}).

Concretely, the split is \textbf{file-level, not window-level}: we held out 13
whole labelled soundscapes and used \emph{no} 5-second window from any held-out
recording anywhere in training. For our own models this exclusion is enforced at
\emph{every} stage, including the soundscape pseudo-labelling that generates
noisy-student targets --- so neither a student nor the teacher that labels its
training data ever sees a held-out file. This is what makes the
ensemble-vs-member comparison of \S\ref{sec:ensemble} clean: both sides of that
comparison are equally held out from the same 13 files. The one exclusion we
\emph{cannot} enforce is on the public components: they were trained by others on
the full labelled set, so when we score them on our hold-out their numbers are
leak-optimistic by construction. That asymmetry --- our side genuinely held out,
the baseline's side not --- is the validation trap of \S\ref{sec:trap}, and it is
why we lean on \emph{relative}, leak-controlled gates (ensemble-vs-best-single;
per-class win counts against the public SED) rather than absolute comparisons to
the baseline.

For each idea we instantiated one of three gates:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{compare\_to\_teacher}} --- train a candidate SED with the
        clean \emph{file-level} hold-out of 13 labelled soundscapes; run the public
        distilled SED on the \emph{same} held-out files; report both macro-AUCs and
        the per-class win count.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{eval\_*\_probe} / \texttt{eval\_rare\_taxa}} --- train
        linear probes on frozen foundation-model embeddings; evaluate against the
        public SED \emph{sliced to specific taxa}; measure orthogonality (how many
        classes the candidate strictly beats the baseline on).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{eval\_sed\_ensemble}} --- measure whether an ensemble's
        macro-AUC exceeds its best single member (a leak-free decorrelation test,
        independent of any public teacher).
\end{itemize}

Each gate ran in minutes on a single cloud GPU at low cost (we did not log a
per-gate cost breakdown; total cloud spend across the study was a few US dollars).
Each falsified direction would otherwise have consumed one-to-many submission slots
and a day of latency. We regard this leak-aware gating discipline
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}) as a small transferable contribution for
code-competitions with daily quotas and slow scoring.

\subsection{An agentic experimentation workflow}
\label{sec:agentic}
This study was conducted as a human-directed, AI-agent-executed workflow: a coding
agent (Anthropic's Claude, driven via Claude Code) carried out the loop of writing
each gate, launching cloud (Modal) training and evaluation jobs, parsing the
results, and proposing the next hypothesis, under human review at each decision
point. The discipline the agent enforced is exactly what made a five-slots-per-day
budget tractable: every hypothesis was turned into a sub-dollar offline measurement
and \emph{gated} before a submission was spent, and dead ends were recorded with
numbers rather than re-litigated by intuition. The agent also caught failures a
human might have shrugged off --- for example, it surfaced that our compare-to-teacher
comparison was leak-biased (\S\ref{sec:trap}) only because it logged and diffed the
per-class win counts. We document this because the organizers explicitly invited
participants to describe AI-assisted and autonomous workflows; in our case the
agentic loop was not a coding convenience but the mechanism that let a small team
exhaustively map the ceiling within the platform's constraints. All experiments
were designed under human direction and every reported number was manually verified
(see the AI-use disclosure before the references).

\subsection{Own-trained SED cannot match the distilled public SED}
\label{sec:ownsed}

We trained EfficientNet-B0 SEDs two ways: focal-recording-only (public LB
\textbf{0.931}) and a noisy-student~\cite{noisystudent} variant on 10{,}592
pseudo-labelled soundscapes $\to$ 127{,}896 soft-labelled windows (public LB
\textbf{0.933}). Both regressed ${\sim}0.017$--$0.019$ below the baseline when
blended in. The gate indicated the cause: on the 13-file hold-out the public
SED scored \textbf{0.994} versus our student's \textbf{0.910} --- though that 0.994
is optimistically biased (\S\ref{sec:trap}). A single own-trained CNN on one GPU
does not reach a model distilled from a strong teacher ensemble --- and, because
our students learn from the public SED's \emph{own} pseudo-labels, they are its
distilled copies, structurally bounded below it~\cite{distillation}.

\subsection{The validation trap: optimistic held-out for public components}
\label{sec:trap}

The gate number above (public SED 0.994 on hold-out) is \textbf{misleading in a way
worth making explicit}, because it determined how we validated everything
afterward. The public Light-ProtoSSM is, by inspection of the public notebook, fit
on the labelled soundscapes (it trains on them in-kernel); the distilled SED was
plausibly distilled with them in scope. Either way, the public pipeline's 0.994 on
a held-out \emph{subset of those same soundscapes} is optimistic by construction,
whereas our genuinely-held-out student is scored fairly --- an asymmetric
comparison. The gap between the public components' ${\sim}0.99$ on labelled audio
and the full pipeline's 0.950 / 0.942 on the hidden test is consistent with this
optimism. The practical consequence: \textbf{an offline comparison of a challenger
against a public component on the labelled soundscapes is biased in the baseline's
favour}, so held-out macro-AUC cannot reliably rank a small team's model against
the shared pipeline. This is, of course, an instance of the textbook
train/test-leakage pitfall; our contribution is not the concept but the
demonstration that, in this competition, it silently invalidates the \emph{most
natural} validation a competitor would reach for, and the consequent discipline of
preferring \emph{leak-controlled relative} gates (e.g.\ ensemble-vs-best-single,
\S\ref{sec:ensemble}) over absolute comparisons to the baseline. (Whether the
precise mechanism is label leakage or in-domain over-fit plus distribution shift,
the methodological conclusion is identical.)

\subsection{Foundation-model probes are strictly dominated}
\label{sec:probes}

Linear probes on Bird-MAE-Base~\cite{birdmae} and AudioMAE~\cite{audiomae}
embeddings beat the public SED on \textbf{0/27} held-out classes with positives;
every rank-blend weight monotonically \emph{reduced} macro-AUC. A
strictly-dominated source cannot improve a calibrated ensemble --- it can only
dilute it.

\subsection{Rare-taxon specialists: a plausible premise, falsified}
\label{sec:raretaxa}

We hypothesized the bird-tuned pipeline would be weak on rare
\emph{Amphibia/Insecta} --- stationary frog/insect textures unlike bird syllables
--- and that a texture-oriented model (AudioMAE~\cite{audiomae}) could win there.
Sliced to 16 measurable rare classes (954 held-out positives), the gate refuted it:
the public SED scored \textbf{0.991} on exactly these taxa (it had learned them from
the labelled soundscapes), and our specialists beat it on \textbf{0/16}
(Bird-MAE 0.876, AudioMAE 0.799 macro-AUC on the slice). External audio cannot
rescue the data-starved tail either: of 53 classes with $<10$ training recordings,
Xeno-Canto (a bird archive) covered 1, and 28 --- including the \texttt{sonXX}
insect ``morphotypes'' --- are competition-internal labels with no scientific name
and thus no external data anywhere.

\subsection{Post-hoc tricks perturb a tight equilibrium}
\label{sec:posthoc}

Genus-proxy boosting, sonotype mirroring, per-taxon temperature, BirdNET 3-way
blending, cross-class co-occurrence boosting, and iterative second-pass smoothing
each layered onto the 0.950 base returned 0.948--0.950 (neutral-to-negative). The
public pipeline's calibration chain (priors, rank-aware scaling, power
optimization, threshold tuning, taxonomy smoothing) is a tight equilibrium;
post-hoc reorderings disturb it more than they help. Tellingly, the community's own
last $+0.001$ came not from \emph{adding} a trick but from \texttt{TAX\_SMOOTHING}
applied \emph{inside} the calibrated chain at the right scale.

\subsection{Diverse-CNN ensembling: a real gain that cannot be deployed}
\label{sec:ensemble}

The most reliable technique in the field is ensembling architecturally-diverse
CNNs, and we used it as a \emph{controlled internal} test rather than a comparison
to the (leak-flattered) baseline. We trained four diverse SED backbones ---
EfficientNetV2-S, ConvNeXt-Small, EfficientNet-B3, SEResNeXt50. Crucially, the 13
hold-out files were excluded from \textbf{all} four members' training,
\emph{including the soundscape pseudo-label generation} (\S\ref{sec:split}) --- so
the ensemble-vs-member comparison is leak-controlled with respect to that split
(both sides are equally held out, unlike the baseline of \S\ref{sec:trap}). On that
single split the four-model rank ensemble scored \textbf{0.9542} versus the best
single member's \textbf{0.9332}, a \textbf{+0.021} improvement --- the expected
diversity effect, observed cleanly. We stress this is a \emph{single-split point
estimate}: we did not run multiple folds or seeds and report no confidence
interval, so $+0.021$ should be read as directional corroboration of the well-known
diversity benefit, not a precise effect size (see \S\ref{sec:threats}).

This still could not break 0.950, for two largely independent reasons that are the
crux of this note. First, \emph{deployment runtime}: adding the four SEDs to the
public pipeline ($5 + 4 = 9$, or even $5 + 2 = 7$ inferences) exceeded the
90-minute budget and timed out --- a wall independent of model quality. Second,
\emph{substitution cost}: swapping two public SEDs for two of ours (holding the
count at 5, runtime-safe) scored \textbf{0.945} on the LB. We read this single
point cautiously: it is \emph{consistent with} our members being individually
weaker than the public SED on the genuine hidden test (where the baseline's leak
advantage vanishes), but it is confounded --- the rank-blend weights were not
re-tuned for the substitution, and our members were only half-trained when a
cloud-budget cap froze them, which independently predicts a drop. The runtime
conclusion is therefore firm; the substitution conclusion is suggestive, not
proven. What the two together establish is narrower but solid: \emph{we could not
realize the diversity gain within the platform envelope}, whether because of
runtime (certain) or insufficient member quality (likely, given half-training) or
both.

\section{The platform-constraint finding}
\label{sec:platform}

The deeper lesson generalizes beyond SEDs. The one strategy with real headroom over
a converged baseline is to \textbf{ensemble multiple full, diverse pipelines}. We
attempted to combine two genuinely different public 0.950 pipelines (distinct
ProtoSSM implementations and auxiliary models) in one notebook. This is blocked by
\emph{platform physics}, not modeling:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Notebook-size cap (${\sim}1$~MB).} The two full pipelines
        concatenated to 1.08~MB and were rejected at upload; a single pipeline is
        0.77~MB.
  \item \textbf{Runtime cap (90~min CPU).} Each pipeline's Perch pass nearly fills
        the budget; two passes time out --- the same wall that killed our
        additive-SED ensembles.
\end{itemize}

These limits are, we argue, \emph{a} structural reason for the monoculture: heavy
foundation-model pipelines cannot be ensembled at inference inside the platform, so
the public field --- which mostly forks the same heavy pipeline --- is funnelled
into a single 0.950 cluster. We then \emph{conjecture} (the leaders' solutions are
private, so this is not established) that the teams above 0.95 escape the funnel by
pre-training their own lighter models that are individually competitive yet cheap
enough to run several within 90 minutes, paying the cost up front in training
compute that a late, small entrant on one consumer GPU cannot match. This
conjecture has at least an empirical prior: published BirdCLEF 2023--2025 winning
solutions~\cite{lifeclef-overview} consistently rely on \emph{ensembles of several
independently-trained CNNs} run within the inference budget, rather than a single
shared backbone. We present it as the most parsimonious hypothesis consistent with
our measurements, not as a demonstrated fact about the 11 teams $\geq 0.96$.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

We state the limits of these conclusions plainly.
(i)~\textbf{Single-split, no error bars.} Our offline gates use one fold and a small
hold-out (13 files; 32 classes with positives for the absolute gates, 16 rare
classes for \S\ref{sec:raretaxa}); we report point estimates without confidence
intervals. The headline $+0.021$ ensemble gain (\S\ref{sec:ensemble}) is therefore
directional corroboration of a known effect, not a measured effect size;
multi-fold/multi-seed validation with CIs is the most important piece of future
work, which our deleted cloud infrastructure prevented us from adding before
submission.
(ii)~\textbf{The leak claim is partly inferential.} We observe that the public
ProtoSSM trains on the labelled soundscapes in-kernel, but only infer the SED's
training scope from the 0.99-vs-0.95 optimism gap; the methodological conclusion
holds under either label leakage or in-domain over-fit plus distribution shift.
(iii)~\textbf{Half-training cuts two ways.} The diverse ensemble was half-trained
when a budget cap froze it. This understates its absolute ceiling (so the
\emph{deployment-wall} conclusion is conservative --- a stronger ensemble would
still time out), but it is also a genuine confound for the \emph{substitution}
result (\S\ref{sec:ensemble}): a fully-trained ensemble might have made the
runtime-safe swap competitive, so we do not claim the swap result proves our
members are intrinsically inferior.
(iv)~\textbf{The leaders are a conjecture.} Our characterization of the $>0.95$
teams (\S\ref{sec:platform}) is unverified --- their solutions are private.
(v)~\textbf{Scope.} All scores use this competition's masked macro-AUC on its
specific data; the \emph{platform-constraint} and \emph{validation-trap} lessons
should transfer qualitatively to similar code-competitions, but the exact
thresholds will not.

%% ===========================================================================
\section{What moved, and final standing}
\label{sec:standing}

The only positive movement available to us was tracking the community's
\texttt{TAX\_SMOOTHING} jump to 0.950 and choosing robust final submissions.
Recognizing the ${\sim}890$-team public tie, we selected two maximally-divergent
0.950 pipelines to hedge the public$\to$private shift. The shift proved mild and
uniform ($0.950 \to \textbf{0.942}$ across our picks); a small robustness signal
favoured the more battle-tested calibration (the older EoS.8 base held 0.942 while
the newer EoS.9 fell to 0.941). Final standing: \textbf{289 / 4243, bronze}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three takeaways we believe are non-obvious and worth recording:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Optimistic held-out can invert your conclusions.} When public
        components are fit on the only in-domain labelled data available for
        validation, offline comparisons systematically flatter them.
        Resource-constrained teams should treat such comparisons as \emph{lower
        bounds} on their own models' relative quality, and prefer leak-free
        relative gates.
  \item \textbf{Leak-aware offline gating.} Validating locally before submitting is
        standard practice --- a local cross-validation split; the twist that
        mattered here is \emph{what} to validate against. Because absolute held-out
        comparison to the baseline is biased (point~1), we gated on \emph{relative}
        and \emph{orthogonality} signals instead (ensemble-vs-best-single; per-class
        win counts against the public SED). This let us reach a decision on five
        directions within the budget a blind approach would spend on one, and we
        recommend the leak-aware variant specifically for code-competitions where
        the only in-domain labels were also used to fit the public baseline.
  \item \textbf{In the foundation-model era the binding constraint is often the
        platform, not the idea.} The highest-value technique (pipeline ensembling)
        and a genuine $+0.021$ ensemble gain were both real and both undeployable
        under a 1~MB / 90-minute envelope. A public ceiling built from large shared
        pretrained components is not a wall of cleverness to out-think; it is a
        floor, and the door through it is pre-training compute a late, small team
        does not have.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together these sharpen the question of where effort actually pays under
foundation-model baselines and platform limits, and suggest that a useful
contribution from a mid-pack team is a measured map of the terrain that winners'
recipes leave unspoken.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We finished 289/4243 (bronze) at private 0.942, absorbed into the public 0.950
monoculture and unable to exceed it. The value of this note is not a score but a
\emph{negative result delivered with measurements}: a falsification of five
improvement directions, identification of a held-out-validation trap, a reusable
leak-aware local-CV gating methodology, and a platform-constraint explanation for
why public foundation-model pipelines form such hard ceilings. We release all code,
the cloud training/evaluation harness, and the gates openly, in the hope the map
helps the next small team decide where to spend its five daily submissions.
